Abstract

AbstractBackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly applied in healthcare to support decision-making, personalize treatment, and improve outcomes. In spine care, AI has been used for both operative and nonoperative interventions, including surgical planning, outcome prediction, and digital tools for chronic low back pain (cLBP). However, evidence remains fragmented and variable in quality, limiting its utility for clinicians and researchers. This review maps the current literature on AI in spinal disorder treatment and highlights gaps for future research.MethodsThis scoping review followed Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) and PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Ovid MEDLINE, AMED, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, Web of Science, and Scopus were searched from January 2019 to December 2024. Eligible studies were English-language, peer-reviewed, involved AI applied to treatment interventions in human participants, included a comparison group, and provided sufficient methodological detail. No geographic restrictions were applied. Studies were evaluated by AI technology, treatment modality, outcomes, and quality. Methodological quality was assessed using a 19-point scoring system covering study design, reporting clarity, data validation, and feature selection.ResultsThe search yielded 1,782 manuscripts; 16 met inclusion criteria. Of these, 3 originated from the United States, 8 were single-country studies, and 5 were international collaborations. Fourteen studies focused on nonoperative management of musculoskeletal pain, particularly cLBP, using chatbots, AI-driven exercise platforms, and decision-support systems. These demonstrated modest improvements in pain, disability, and quality of life, with high adherence and satisfaction. Two studies investigated operative applications, reporting favorable results. Methodological scores ranged from 8.5 to 17/19, with common limitations in data validation and feature selection.ConclusionsCurrent literature demonstrates AI applications in nonoperative management of cLBP and in operative contexts such as surgical planning and outcome prediction. Most studies addressed cLBP, with limited exploration of neck pain, highlighting an area for future investigation.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Spine care, Chronic low back pain, Digital health, Chatbots, Self-management applications, Exercise therapy, Clinical decision support, Machine learning, Operative and nonoperative interventions

Background

The recent evolution of artificial intelligence (AI) has reshaped technologies across many domains, placing healthcare at the forefront of this transformation [1]. AI’s unique capacity to integrate vast and complex data, learn from patterns, and generate predictive insights, positions it as an invaluable tool for optimizing clinical workflows [2] and supporting personalized treatment strategies [3]. In particular, the recent surge in AI innovation, driven by advances in machine learning, deep learning, neural networks, and natural language processing [4], has aided in the development of sophisticated tools that could be utilized for clinical purposes [5,6]. AI has demonstrated some utility in diagnosing diseases, developing personalized treatment plans, and assisting with clinical decision-making [5,7].

From preoperative risk assessment and surgical planning to rehabilitation and chronic pain management, AI-driven solutions emerge at multiple stages along the treatment continuum. Both operative procedures such as decompression, fusion, and device implantation [8,9], and nonoperative interventions including medication management, physical therapy, and behavioral therapies [10], present opportunities for AI to address the complex and multifactorial nature of pain management in spine care [11].

Given the high prevalence, chronicity, and socioeconomic burden of spinal disorders worldwide, the field of spine care is pivotal for addressing this public health burden [[12], [13], [14]]. Conditions such as low back pain, degenerative disc disease, and spinal stenosis account for substantial healthcare utilization and disability [15]. Due to the fact that underlying pathology, comorbidities, and recovery trajectories vary widely, individualized treatment plans are essential. Individualized treatment plans are critical in demonstrating that tailoring surgical approaches, implant choices, and rehabilitation strategies to patient-specific factors improves outcomes and minimizes complications [16]. Another more recent editorial highlighted the value of smartphone-based mobility tracking to guide personalized follow-up and support a shift toward value-based care [17]. The integration of AI into spine care settings has gained significant attention in recent years as emerging research demonstrates AI’s ability to enhance diagnostic accuracy, support clinical decision-making, and streamline care pathways [[18], [19], [20]]. Despite these encouraging findings, a comprehensive understanding of how AI has been specifically applied to spine care treatment across both surgical and nonoperative modalities remains limited.

This scoping review focuses on evaluating the current landscape of AI applications in spine care treatment, encompassing both operative and nonoperative interventions. The objectives are to identify the types of AI technologies being utilized, assess their role in treatment planning, and highlight key challenges such as data quality, model validation, and clinical utility.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews and follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines.

Search Strategy

A comprehensive search strategy was developed by a librarian (AB) to identify relevant studies on the application of AI in spine treatment. The MEDLINE strategy was reviewed by a senior librarian using PRESS [21] before the additional databases were searched. The search focused on literature published between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2024, to capture the most recent advancements. Searches were conducted across the following electronic databases: Ovid MEDLINE, AMED, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, Web of Science-Core Collection, and Scopus. Search terms combining AI concepts (e.g., ``machine learning,'' ``deep learning,'' ``neural networks,'' ``natural language processing'') with spine care and treatment-related keywords. Controlled vocabulary was also used when applicable. The only filter applied was to limit the publication years. All identified citations were deduplicated using the Yale University Harvey Cushing/John Hay Whitney Medical Library Reference Deduplicator tool prior to import and then screening in Covidence. See supplemental material for full search string.

Source Evidence Selection

Following deduplication, 3 independent reviewers (VB, MB, AH) screened titles and abstracts for eligibility. Full-text articles were retrieved for studies that met the inclusion criteria or where eligibility was unclear. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion (VB, MB, AH). The study selection process was documented using a PRISMA flow diagram.

Inclusion/Exclusion

Studies were included if they investigated AI technologies applied to treatment modalities in spine care, including both operative and nonoperative interventions. Eligible studies involved human participants and compared AI-based approaches to non-AI methods or benchmarked them against established standards. Studies focusing on administrative, financial, or nonclinical applications of AI were excluded, as were studies on animals, cadavers, or those without a clinical decision-making component. Data Extraction

A sample manuscript extraction was performed independently by 2 reviewers (EB, VB) using a structured extraction template, achieving an >85 % level of agreement. For the remaining studies, extraction was completed by a single reviewer (EB). Extracted data included study characteristics (author, year, country, design, population), AI technology type, comparison group, treatment modality (operative or nonoperative), outcome measures (e.g., patient-reported outcomes, treatment effectiveness), and key findings. For outcome measures, we specifically extracted validated patient-reported outcome metrics where available, including but not limited to the following, pain intensity scales (e.g., Numerical Rating Scale, Visual Analog Scale) [22,23], disability indices (e.g., Oswestry Disability Index, Roland-Morris Disability Questionnaire) [24,25], health-related quality-of-life measures (e.g., EQ-5D, EQ-VAS) [26,27], and PROMIS domains [28].

Data Analysis

Extracted manuscripts were synthesized narratively and presented in tabular form. Studies were categorized based on treatment modality, type of AI technology, and reported outcomes. Comparisons between AI and non-AI approaches were emphasized, with attention to whether studies reported clinical utility, patient and clinician outcomes, and identified research gaps. While citation counts are sometimes used as a proxy for utility or impact, they were not included in this review because they primarily reflect dissemination rather than methodological rigor or clinical effectiveness, and they are strongly influenced by time since publication.

Quality Assessment

While formal quality appraisal is not required in scoping reviews, we conducted a structured quality assessment to contextualize the methodological rigor of included studies. We developed a custom checklist adapted from the APPRAISE-AI tool [29], and the TRIPOD-AI extension [30]. This tool evaluates key elements of AI-based clinical research, including data representativeness, transparency, bias mitigation, model performance, and reporting practices (Fig. 1).

Results

A total of 1,782 manuscripts were identified through searches across 6 databases. After removing 615 duplicates, 1,167 manuscripts were screened by title and abstract. Ultimately, 16 studies met all criteria and were included in the final review, comprising 3 studies conducted in the United States, 8 single-country studies outside the United States, and 5 international collaborations across multiple countries.

Sixteen studies were included in the review, spanning randomized controlled trials (RCT) [[31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37], [38]], cohort [39] and longitudinal studies [40], secondary analyses [[41], [42], [43], [44]], predictive modeling [45], and system validation research [46] conducted across multiple countries. All studies utilized supervised AI methods. The majority of studies addressed nonoperative management of low back pain musculoskeletal pain, using AI-based digital health interventions such as chatbots, self-management mobile applications, exercise coaching platforms, and machine learning–driven decision support systems [[31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37],[40], [41], [42], [43], [44],46,47]. These tools commonly provided personalized exercise prescriptions, patient education, cognitive-behavioral strategies, and real-time feedback, often delivered through smartphones or wearables, with intervention durations ranging from 2 weeks to twelve months. A smaller number of studies examined operative contexts, including AI and augmented reality (AR) applications for patient selection and intraoperative navigation in spinal cord stimulation and sacroplasty [39,45] (Table 3).

Across the 16 included studies, patient-reported outcomes consistently demonstrated that AI-based interventions for low back pain yielded clinically meaningful improvements in pain, disability, and quality of life, though the magnitude and durability of effects varied. Standardized outcome measures included pain intensity scales (e.g., Numerical Rating Scale, Visual Analog Scale), disability indices (e.g., Oswestry Disability Index, Roland-Morris Disability Questionnaire), and quality-of-life instruments (e.g., EQ-5D, EQ-VAS), as detailed in Table 4. Workplace and mobile application based chatbot systems reduced pain severity and improved global ratings of change with high adherence [31,33]. Digital self-management platforms such as SELFBACK and medical motion reported significant but modest reductions in disability and pain compared with usual care, with benefits sustained up to 9 months in some cohorts [38,40, [42], [43], [44]]. Trials of AI-driven exercise systems and decision-support tools demonstrated improvements in function, pain interference, and kinesiophobia, with adherence rates exceeding two-thirds of prescribed sessions [32,41]. In contrast, multimodal and long-term app-based interventions such as Rise-uP achieved larger effect sizes across pain, disability, psychological health, and healthcare utilization, with added evidence for cost-effectiveness [37].

Validation of AI algorithms was heterogeneous. While most studies reported effectiveness based on clinical outcomes without formal external testing, some provided algorithmic performance metrics. For example, predictive models for spinal cord stimulation achieved strong discriminatory accuracy (AUC 0.78–0.83) and outperformed invasive lead trialing [45], while a deep learning platform for digital physical therapy reported >90 % accuracy for kinematic analysis [35]. Conversely, reinforcement learning and human-in-the-loop systems demonstrated adaptive feasibility but lacked independent validation datasets [36,41].

Clinician satisfaction and usability were infrequently evaluated in a systematic manner. Most interventions functioned as patient-facing tools with minimal clinician involvement, though indirect usability signals, such as high adherence, scalable therapist capacity, and patient satisfaction, were commonly observed [36,41]. Where assessed, digital therapy platforms demonstrated favorable ratings for accessibility, feedback, and cost [35], while integration challenges were noted in multimodal care pathways [37].

Quality Assessment Ratings

Across the 16 included studies, reporting quality was generally strong in domains related to sample size, representativeness, and transparency, as well as in target variable definition and baseline model comparisons, which were consistently well described. All studies adhered to privacy and consent compliance standards, and nearly all provided financial disclosures. By contrast, important gaps were evident in the documentation of missing data handling, bias identification and mitigation, and feature selection methods, which were reported in fewer than half of studies. Furthermore, data element validation was rarely performed, and data availability beyond trial contexts was limited. Discussion

This scoping review provides an overview of recent applications of artificial intelligence in spine care, covering both operative and nonoperative interventions. Across the included studies, AI was applied to support personalized treatment plans, monitor patient progress, and assist clinical decision-making. Nonoperative interventions such as chatbots, exercise applications, and digital self-management platforms showed consistent though modest benefits in pain reduction, disability, and quality of life. Operative applications, including predictive models for spinal cord stimulation and augmented reality–guided procedures, demonstrated promising feasibility and accuracy in early evaluations.

Of the 16 included studies, 3 were conducted in the United States, 8 were single-country studies conducted outside the United States, and 5 were multinational collaborations. This distribution reflects the strong international interest in AI for spine care but also highlights the relatively small number of U.S.-based investigations. In light of this the current evidence base remains highly heterogeneous. Studies varied widely in design, sample size, intervention type, AI methodology, and outcome measures, limiting direct comparison. While several trials reported clinically meaningful improvements, others found no significant benefit over standard care. Furthermore, most studies did not include external validation of algorithms, and long-term outcomes were infrequently assessed, raising concerns about the durability and generalizability of observed effects. Similar challenges have been noted in prior reviews, with Shi reporting that most AI spine models were trained on small, single-center datasets with limited validation [48], and Quinones highlighting the lack of standardized outcome measures across studies [49].

An important trend is that AI appeared most impactful when used to augment clinical judgment rather than replace it. Human-in-the-loop systems and multimodal care pathways achieved higher adherence, better patient satisfaction, and more sustainable improvements than standalone applications. Our findings are supported by related reviews, that emphasized that AI performs best when integrated into clinical workflows to enhance decision-making [50], and noted that multimodal pathways improve scalability and adherence [51]. These observations suggest that embedding AI within established clinical frameworks may offer the greatest potential for advancing spine care.

The rapid growth of AI in healthcare, as in other areas, has raised significant concerns surrounding data privacy, ethical implications, and regulatory challenges. Apprehensions related to data ownership, model stewardship, data/model bias, model transparency/interpretability, and more are emerging topics and under debate [[52], [53], [54], [55], [56]]. The concerns are related to patient privacy, data drift, potential for model gaming, model bias unfairly targeting marginalized populations, and inequitable access to quality care. Guidelines aimed at reducing risk in AI systems have been put forth by the US Federal Trade Commission [57], the European Union [58], China [59], and industry and professional stakeholders [60]. As AI becomes more integrated into healthcare delivery and operations, the discussions regarding ethical and effective use of AI will grow.

Overall, AI-based interventions show potential to enhance personalization, adherence, and scalability in musculoskeletal pain management. Yet, current evidence underscores the need for standardized outcomes, consistent follow-up, and transparent reporting of AI design and validation. Building trust will require rigorous evaluation, explainability, and attention to safety, equity, and clinical integration. Importantly, research shows that clinicians’ willingness to adopt AI depends not only on its technical performance but also on perceptions of risk, transparency, and clinical autonomy [61,62]. While some studies demonstrate that positive expectations and perceived utility encourage adoption, others highlight that lack of training and concerns over algorithmic opacity remain major barriers [61,62]. Future work should therefore prioritize long-term, patient outcome–driven evaluations across diverse settings, supported by rigorous external validation and explainable AI. Addressing these methodological and perceptual gaps will be critical to ensure safety, comparability, and meaningful integration of AI into spine care.

Limitations

This review was limited to English-language, peer-reviewed studies published between 2019 and 2024, potentially omitting relevant non-English or earlier research. Considerable heterogeneity in AI technologies, treatment modalities, patient populations, and outcome measures precluded direct comparison across studies. Variability in follow-up periods and outcome reporting further limited comparability. While a structured quality assessment was performed, formal risk-of-bias appraisal was not undertaken, and advances in AI may render some findings outdated relative to current technology.

Conclusions

This review provides a mapping of the literature on artificial intelligence in the management of spinal disorders. The most commonly reported theme was nonoperative management of chronic low back pain conditions. Overall, the current evidence is mixed, with modest effect sizes, variable follow-up, and limited external validation of algorithms. Future research should prioritize standardized outcomes, longer-term evaluation, and transparent reporting to confirm effectiveness and ensure safe, equitable integration into spine care.
