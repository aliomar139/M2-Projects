Abstract
Background When faced with a severe intraarticular in-
jury like a tibial plateau fracture, patients count on surgeons 
to make an accurate estimation of prognosis. 
Unfortunately, there are few tools available that enable 
precise, personalized prognosis estimation tailored to each 
patient’s unique circumstances, including their individual 
and fracture-speciﬁc characteristics. In this study, we de-
veloped and validated a clinical prediction model using 
machine-learning algorithms for the 2- and 5-year risk of 
TKA after tibia plateau fractures.
Questions/purposes Can machine learning–based proba-
bility calculators estimate the probability of 2- and 5-year
risk of conversion to TKA in patients with a tibial plateau 
fracture?
Methods A multicenter, cross-sectional study was per-
formed in six hospitals in patients treated for a tibial plateau 
fracture between 2003 to 2019. In total, 2057 patients were 
eligible for inclusion and were sent informed consent and 
a questionnaire to inquire whether they underwent con-
version to TKA. For 56% (1160 of 2057), status of con-
version to TKA was accounted for at a minimum of 2 years, 
and 53% (1082 of 2057) were accounted for at a minimum 
of 5 years. The mean follow-up among responders was 6 6
4 years after injury. An analysis of nonresponders found 
that responders were slightly older than nonresponders

Results There were no differences among the models in 
terms of sensitivity and speciﬁcity; the AUCs for each 
overlapped broadly and ranged from 0.76 to 0.83. 
Calibration was most optimal in logistic regression for both 
2- and 5-year models, with slopes of 0.82 (random forest 
0.60, XGBoost 0.26) and 0.95 (random forest 0.85, 
XGBoost 0.48) and intercepts of 0.01 for both (random 
forest 0.01 to 0.02; XGBoost 0.05 to 0.07). Brier score was 
similar between models varying between 0.06 to 0.09. 
Given that its performance metrics were highest, we chose 
the logistic regression algorithm as the ﬁnal prediction 
model. The web application providing the prediction tool is 
freely available and can be accessed through: https:// 
3dtrauma.shinyapps.io/tka_prediction/.
Conclusion In this study, a personalized risk assessment 
tool was developed to support clinical decision-making and 
patient counseling. Our ﬁndings demonstrate that machine-
learning algorithms, particularly logistic regression, can 
provide accurate and reliable predictions of TKA conver-
sion at 2 and 5 years after a tibial plateau fracture. In ad-
dition, it provides a useful prognostic tool for surgeons who 
perform fracture surgery that can be used quickly and 
easily with patients in the clinic or emergency department 
once it complies with medical device regulations. External 
validation is needed to assess performance in other insti-
tutions and countries; to account for patient and surgeon 
preferences, resources, and cultures; and to further 
strengthen its clinical applicability.

Introduction
Lower leg injuries  have a major impact  on patient ’s 
mobility,  social  activities , and ability  to work. In par-
ticular,  injuries  to the load-bearing  part of the knee, in-
cluding  tibial  plateau  fractu res, may substantially  impair  
movement  [10]. The incidence  of tibial  plateau  fractures  
is 10 to 25 per 100,000  people  [9, 12], which means that 
this injury  affects  about 2 million  individuals  worldwide  
each year. Especially  in cities,  where millions  of people  
ride motorcycles,  scooters,  and bicycles,  these injuries  
are common,  and many are treated  surgically.  But de-
spite achieving  anatomic  reduction,  damage  to the tibial  
plateau  might progress  to posttraumatic  osteoarthritis  
and instability,  resulting  in pain and stiffness,  and 
sometimes  to a conversion  to TKA [17, 18]. Compared  
with the general  population,  tibial  plateau  fractures  are 
associated  with a 3.5- to 5-times  increased  risk of 
TKA [8, 32].

Patients with these injuries often face difﬁcult deci-
sions about their treatment options, and shared decision-
making is (or should be) a crucial aspect of their care. 
Patients may reasonably  harbor uncertainty  about injury 
severity, beneﬁts and harms of surgery, and prognosis. 
However, estimating  prognosis is challenging  because so 
many factors can inﬂuence patients’ results after these 
injuries, including patient characteristics  (such as age, 
gender, BMI, comorbidities),  fracture pattern, and the 
alignment of the fracture on preoperative  and post-
operative radiographs  [1, 26, 32]. With so many variables 
to consider, accurate prediction  of clinical outcomes after 
treatment remains challenging.  Moreover, estimating  
prognosis often is subjective  and relies heavily on the 
surgeon’s experience as much or more than data-driven  
insights. Machine learning–based algorithms  have the 
potential to combine different features into one model and 
show great potential in terms of fracture detection, grad-
ing osteoarthritis,  and predicting outcomes like mortality 
[6, 13, 23]. These algorithms could be valuable as well in 
predicting clinical outcome after injuries. Such a model 
could beneﬁt patient counseling  and shared decision-
making, as it would allow for more personalized  and in-
formed treatment plans. However, currently we lack good 
clinical prediction models in orthopaedic  trauma surgery. 
Existing outcome prediction models in this ﬁeld are lim-
ited to mortality or length of hospital stay [20, 25]. We 
therefore present an innovative  proof of concept for de-
veloping clinical prediction  models in orthopaedic  
trauma, aiming to establish a baseline work for outcome 
prediction after injuries. For this purpose, we collected 
clinical outcomes from a large, multicenter  cohort of 
patients with tibial plateau fractures.  Our goal was to 
develop and internally  validate a machine learning–based 
prediction model utilizing patient characteristics  for 
a patient-speci ﬁc risk assessment  of 2- and 5-year con-
version to TKA. Additionally,  we aimed to integrate the 
algorithm into an online, freely accessible prediction  tool 
that can eventually be utilized at the point of care once it 
complies with medical device regulations.
In this study, we asked: Can machine learning–based 
probability calculators estimate the probability of 2- and 5-
year risk of conversion to TKA in patients with a tibial 
plateau fracture?
Patients and Methods
This study is reported following the Transparent Reporting 
of Multivariable Prediction Models for Individual 
Prognosis or Diagnosis Guideline (TRIPOD statement 
[Appendix 1; http://links.lww.com/CORR/B400]) and the
Journal of Medical Internet Research Guidelines for 
Developing and Reporting Machine Learning Predictive
Models in Biomedical Research [5, 16]. A study protocol 
was not published.
Study Design and Setting
We performed a multicenter, cross-sectional study in 
patients treated for tibial plateau fractures in the ortho-
paedic and trauma surgery department of six hospitals 
within The Netherlands and Belgium between 2003 to 
2019. Hospitals consisted of two academic centers and four 
nonacademic centers in medium-sized cities.
Patients
We identiﬁed 2241 patients who had an available di-
agnostic CT scan and who were still alive according to the 
national population registry. Adult patients ($ 18 years 
old) who were treated operatively or nonoperatively for 
their tibial plateau fracture were potentially eligible. From 
the identiﬁed patients, we excluded 0.2% (4 of 2241) be-
cause they required amputation, and we excluded 4% (89 
of 2241) because of coexisting conditions complicating 
outcome measurement, including Parkinson disease, ce-
rebrovascular accident, and paralysis. In addition, we ex-
cluded 3% (60 of 2241) of patients because their address 
was unknown, and we excluded 1% (31 of 2241) because 
they spoke a foreign language. Subsequently, 2057 patients 
were eligible for inclusion and were sent informed consent 
and questionnaires by posted mail to inquire whether they 
underwent conversion to TKA. In case of no response, 
a reminder was sent. Fifty-six percent (1160 of 2057) of 
patients responded at 6 6 4 years after injury (Fig. 1). For 
ﬁfty-six percent (1160 of 2057), status of conversion to 
TKA was accounted for at a minimum of 2 years, and 53% 
(1082 of 2057) were accounted for at a minimum of
5 years.
Analysis of Nonresponders
For the nonresponse analysis, we used an independent-
samples t-test for continuous variables and a chi-square test 
for noncontinuous variables. Overall, nonresponders did 
not differ very much from responders, which tends to lend 
credibility to the analysis. The nonresponse analysis 
showed that nonresponders were slightly younger than 
responders (mean 6 SD 51 6 17 years versus 53 6 
16 years; p = 0.001), there was a smaller proportion of 
women (58% [523 of 897] versus 68% [788 of 1160]; p = 
0.001), and they received nonoperative treatment more 
often (43% [387 of 897] versus 30% [346 of 1160]; p = 
0.001). Knee radiographs and CT images of nonresponders

Primary Study Outcome
The primary outcome of the model was conversion to TKA 
at 2- and 5-year follow-up. Whether and when a patient 
underwent conversion to a TKA was collected by 
approaching all patients. These time points were selected 
because the conversion rate to TKA is particularly high 
within the ﬁrst 5 years after fracture [11] and because of the 
availability of reliable data.
Data Cleaning and Feature Selection
Obtained features, derived from follow-up questionnaires, 
electronic patient records, and radiographic assessments, 
were eligible for development of the prediction model 
(Table 1). Data cleaning included simple type formatting 
(for example, converting “yes”/“no” columns into 
Booleans) and included standardization of numeric col-
umns. Subsequent feature selection consisted of an evi-
dence review according to the methodological framework 
principles as proposed by Levac et al. [14] and expert 
opinion. This was followed by bivariate analysis of the 
identiﬁed features. Following the rule of thumb for build-
ing prediction models with a binary outcome, we limited 
feature selection to ensure that at least 10 events (TKAs) for 
each feature were included within the model. Feature se-
lection resulted in the following included features: age, 
gender, BMI, AO/OTA classiﬁcation, initial gap, initial 
step-off, medial proximal tibial alignment (MPTA), and 
posterior proximal tibial alignment (PPTA).
Missing Data
There were no missing values other than for BMI. For BMI, 
4% (52 of 1160) of patients had missing data. Missing 
values were imputed through K-nearest neighbors impu-
tation. K-nearest neighbors imputation was chosen to in-
crease biological feasibility of the imputations by basing 
them on similar cases.

[3, 4]. To capture potential nonlinear relationships in the 
data, we incorporated restricted cubic splines into the lo-
gistic regression model, allowing for greater ﬂexibility 
while avoiding overﬁtting. Logistic regression predicts the 
probability of an outcome based on a linear relationship 
between features and is valued for its simplicity and in-
terpretability. In contrast, random forest combines multiple

Models were tested using sixfold cross-validation by 
training the model on data from ﬁve (of six) respective

classiﬁcation, every cross-validation fold used a subset of 
the training set to determine the classiﬁcation threshold. 
This approach qualiﬁes as external validation because the 
model’s performance is assessed in sixfold on data from 
a distinct medical center that was not included during 
training, simulating its ability to generalize to unseen 
clinical environments.
Assessment of Model Performance
We used sixfold cross-validation to compare the models. 
Accuracy was deemed an inappropriate measure for per-
formance given that the highly imbalanced nature of the 
data set allows an accuracy of 90% just from predicting the 
false label (that is, if the model always predicts no con-
version to TKA, it will be right 90% of the time, as TKA 
within 5 years only occurred in 10% of the patients). 
Instead, we chose the area under the receiver operating 
characteristic curve (AUC) as the main performance met-
ric. The AUC is widely used to measure accuracy of di-
agnostic tests and provides an aggregate measure of 
performance across all possible classiﬁcation thresholds. 
The AUC score ranges from 0 to 1, with a score of 0.5 
indicating random guessing and 1 indicating perfect per-
formance. To ensure robust and reliable results, we used 
bootstrapping as a resampling technique to estimate the 
variability of the AUC. This method generates conﬁdence 
intervals (CIs) by repeatedly sampling from the data set 
with replacement, helping to assess model stability and 
mitigate overﬁtting, especially when dealing with limited 
data. In addition, calibration curves were plotted, and cal-
ibration was assessed with the calibration slope and in-
tercept. The calibration plot compares the estimated 
probabilities with the observed probabilities for the pri-
mary outcome. Calibration slope assesses how well pre-
dicted probabilities align with observed outcomes (1 = 
perfect, < 1 = overﬁtting, > 1 = underﬁtting), and the cal-
ibration intercept indicates overall prediction bias (0 = 
perfect, negative = underestimation, positive = over-
estimation). Last, the Brier score was calculated. The Brier 
score quantiﬁes the accuracy of probabilistic predictions by 
measuring the mean squared difference between predicted 
probabilities and actual outcomes (0 = perfect).

The logistic regression algorithm was chosen as the ﬁnal

Discussion
Despite achieving anatomic reduction, damage to the ar-
ticular surface of the tibial plateau can progress to post-
traumatic osteoarthritis and instability, leading to pain, 
stiffness, and, in some instances, patients’ query for con-
version to TKA. Patients with these injuries often face 
complex decisions about their index treatment options,
making shared decision-making a crucial aspect of their 
care. However, with numerous variables to consider, ac-
curately predicting clinical outcomes after treatment 
remains a signiﬁcant challenge. Prognosis estimation is 
often ﬂawed by surgeon biases rather than data-driven 
insights. Currently, such robust clinical prediction models 
in orthopaedic trauma surgery to assist in shared decision-
making are scarce. In this study, we developed a machine 
learning–driven model that provides personalized outcome 
predictions for each respective patient based on speciﬁc 
patient and fracture characteristics. This approach advan-
ces prior research that primarily focused on associations 
between individual features and the risk of TKA [1, 2, 30]. 
Our model predicts the likelihood of conversion to TKA 
following a tibial plateau fracture and is integrated into an 
easy-to-use web application. The tool is designed with 
robust modeling and is intended at future use at the point of 
care, but it must ﬁrst comply with medical device regu-
lations. Further validation will be critical for further 
strengthening its clinical applicability.
Limitations
Our developed model was compromised by several limi-
tations that arose from the study setup. First, selection bias 
caused by loss to follow-up of nonresponders is inherent to 
the cross-sectional study design. By contacting all patients 
multiple times, we minimized the risk of nonresponse bias 
as much as possible. Our overall response rate of 56% 
aligns with expectations for postal surveys based on 
existing evidence [21]. Nonresponse analysis showed that 
nonresponders were predominantly patients with relatively

Discussion of Key Findings
In this study, we developed a personalized risk assessment 
tool using patient and fracture characteristics to support 
clinical decision-making and patient counseling. Our 
ﬁndings demonstrate that machine learning–driven prob-
ability calculators, particularly logistic regression, can 
provide accurate and reliable predictions of TKA conver-
sion at 2 and 5 years after a tibial plateau fracture. In 
a clinical setting, this model (freely available at https:// 
3dtrauma.shinyapps.io/tka_prediction/) is intended for use 
in shared decision-making, which eventually could result 
in a better informed patient whose expectations regarding 
expected outcome could be better managed. However, it 
must ﬁrst meet medical device regulations before being 
approved for clinical use. Additionally, this prediction 
model might also guide discussions between professionals 
regarding surgical treatment plans, including considering 
primary TKA or minimizing surgical exposure as a bridge 
to secondary TKA in patients with a high probability of 
conversion to TKA after osteosynthesis [28, 29]. While our 
model focuses on predicting clinical outcomes, most 
existing outcome prediction models in orthopaedic trauma 
are limited to mortality or length of hospital stay [7]. One 
recent study developed a machine-learning model to pre-
dict 2-year conversion to arthroplasty after a femoral neck 
fracture but performed poorly with AUCs of 0.57 to 0.64 
and calibration slopes of 0.53 to 0.82 [31]. Another study
sought to predict the risk of total knee replacement within 
5 years in patients presenting with recent knee pain and 
showed good discrimination and calibration with an AUC 
of 0.79 to 0.87 and calibration slope of 0.95 to 0.96 [15]. 
Similarly, our best-performing models achieved AUCs of 
0.79 and 0.82 for the 2- and 5-year predictions, re-
spectively, with calibration slopes of 0.82 and 0.95, in-
dicating comparable performance to the previously 
mentioned model. This model forms a reasonable basis for 
making prognostic estimates; however, external validation 
across diverse regions and healthcare systems is necessary 
to fully assess its robustness and applicability before 
implementation.
Our study showed that a relatively simple model such as 
logistic regression performed better than more complex 
models such as XGBoost. Simpler models are usually more 
resistant to issues like relatively small sample size, whereas 
complex models are prone to overﬁtting on small data sets 
[24]. The addition of the cubic splines further improved the 
model by more ﬂexible relationships between the predictor 
variables and the outcome, particularly capturing nonlinear 
effects of age or BMI where the impact on the outcome may 
vary at different stages. Our ﬁnal logistic regression model 
delivers robust and reliable predictions despite the rela-
tively small data set. Also, by using these explainable 
models, physicians can eventually make better treatment 
decisions based on data. When comparing our data set with 
different prediction models in orthopaedic trauma research, 
our model is based on more patients than the median 
sample size of 875 within these studies [7], indicating that, 
despite the relatively small data set, it is among the larger 
data sets in orthopaedic trauma research. Future reﬁnement 
of the model could involve the creation of larger, pro-
spectively collected data sets, which would enable the use 
of more complex models and improve the accuracy and 
predictive power of the tool. This would enhance its ability 
to capture nuanced relationships within the data and offer 
even more precise predictions for TKA conversion, al-
though the personalized risk should always be interpreted 
by the physician and considered alongside professional 
experience and the patient’s wishes.
Conclusion
In this study, we developed and validated a machine lear-
ning–based prediction model (https://3dtrauma.shinyapps. 
io/tka_prediction/) for identifying patients at risk of 
conversion to a TKA within 2 and 5 years after a tibial 
plateau fracture. Using patient and fracture characteristics, 
we developed a personalized risk assessment tool to 
support clinical decision-making and patient counseling, 
and we believe that integrating machine learning into 
clinical practice can help manage expectations and enable
