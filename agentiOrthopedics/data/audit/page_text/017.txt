Prognostic Research
Predicting patient outcomes and risk 
for revision surgery after hip and knee 
replacement surgery: study protocol 
for a comparison of modelling approaches 
using the Swiss National Joint Registry (SIRIS)
Léonie Hofstetter1,2,3, Nathalie Schweyckart1, Christof Seiler4,5,6, Christian Brand7, Laura C. Rosella8, 
Mazda Farshad3, Milo A. Puhan2 and Cesar A. Hincapié1,2,3*   
Abstract 
Background Prediction of postoperative patient-reported outcomes and risk for revision surgery after total hip 
arthroplasty (THA) or total knee arthroplasty (TKA) can inform clinical decision-making, health resource allocation, 
and care planning. Machine learning (ML) algorithms are increasingly used as an alternative to traditional logistic 
regression (LR) prediction, but there is uncertainty about their superiority in overall model performance. The aim 
of this study is to compare the predictive performance of LR with different ML approaches for predicting patient out-
comes and risk for revision surgery after THA and TKA.
Methods A population-based historical cohort study will be developed using routinely collected data from all 
primary and revision THA and TKA procedures performed in Switzerland and registered in the Swiss National Joint 
Registry (SIRIS). Patients of age ≥ 18 years with surgery for primary osteoarthritis from 01 January 2015 up to 31 
December 2023 will be included. Outcomes of interest will be (1) 12-month postoperative poor pain outcome 
(defined as < 50% improvement of pain or < 3 absolute reduction in pain on a 11-point (0 to 10) numeric rating scale) 
and poor satisfaction outcome, and (2) early revision within 5 years after primary surgery. Prespecified predictor vari-
ables will include demographic characteristics, comorbidity score, patient-reported health status measures, and surgi-
cal variables. Measures of overall predictive accuracy, discrimination, and calibration will be used to compare predic-
tive performance, and decision curve analysis performed to evaluate the clinical usefulness of models. The models will 
be internally validated using cross-validation and externally validated using geographical validation. Development 
of the models will be informed by the updated Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Indi-
vidual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD + AI) statement.
Discussion This study will develop, validate, and compare prediction models for postoperative patient-reported 
outcomes and risk for revision surgery after THA and TKA using SIRIS data.

Background
Total hip arthroplasty (THA) and total knee arthro -
plasty (TKA) are commonly performed surgical 
procedures in Switzerland [1 ]. When conservative, 
non-surgical treatments prove ineffective in managing 
arthritis symptoms, these procedures provide patients 
with improved quality of life and function [2 ]. The 
Swiss National Joint Registry (SIRIS) was implemented 
in 2012, and now records about 98% of all hip and knee 
arthroplasties and accounts for an estimated 22,000 hip 
joint and 18,000 knee joint replacements each year [3 ]. 
While joint replacements typically have an expected 
lifespan of at least 25 years on average, a small propor -
tion will fail within a shorter time frame and result in 
poor response outcome or require revision surgery [4 , 
5]. Despite being highly successful with respect to pros -
thesis-related outcomes, about 7% to 23% of patients 
report unfavorable pain outcome after THA, and 10% 
to 34% after TKA [6 ]. Premature revision surgery 
imposes significant challenges for both patients, health 
systems, and society [7 ].
Shared decision-making, where clinicians and patients 
share available evidence and preferences to support 
upcoming treatment decisions, is known to improve 
care and costs [8]. Providing patients and clinicians with 
useful and accurate predictions of postoperative out -
comes can facilitate communication and shared deci -
sion-making. By capturing patient perspectives on their 
health status in a standardized way, patient-reported 
outcome measures (PROMs) are considered important 
tools to evaluate treatment outcomes [9] and promote 
healthcare quality improvement [10]. Predicting PROMs 
and the risk of revision surgery may enhance a patient’s 
awareness of potential risks associated with elective sur -
gery, and also facilitate clinicians’ and hospitals’ ability 
to anticipate postoperative courses, healthcare burden, 
resource allocation, and care planning [11, 12].
There is growing interest in the development and use 
of clinical prediction models where decisions regard -
ing treatment choices and plans of management are 
informed by an individual’s profile of prognostic factors 
[13]. While traditional regression-based risk prediction 
models, for example logistic regression (LR), are widely 
used, machine learning approaches are increasingly being 
used as an alternative method [14, 15]. Machine learning 
models can directly and automatically learn from new 
data and make decisions or predictions without being 
explicitly programmed to perform specific tasks [16].
Supervised machine learning models use algorithms 
that are trained to learn from data in  situations when 
the outcome of interest is specified and then make deci -
sions based on similar characteristics or variables from 
new data [17]. They are considered powerful tools that 
may support clinical decision-making by their ability to 
integrate a large amount of data and to identify com -
plex patterns [18]. Despite the possible advantages of 
machine learning models, there is uncertainty whether 
they have superior predictive performance compared 
to traditional methods such as logistic regression, and 
more research with better reporting of both types of 
algorithms are needed to better compare their per -
formance [19]. It has been estimated that only 40% of 
machine learning models developed for predicting out -
comes after THA or TKA have been evaluated in the 
context of traditional statistical prediction techniques 
such as logistic regression, and only 30% of studies used 
external validation [20].
Among machine learning approaches, random forest 
(RF) and gradient-boosted machines such as extreme 
gradient boosting (XGBoost)—both regression-based 
decision trees—are most commonly used in the area 
of THA and TKA prediction modelling and are well 
known for their good predictive performance [20]. 
Despite possible advantages, there is little evidence 
for their adoption in clinical practice [21]. Some limi -
tations are that concepts such as machine learning are 
still novel to many health researchers and clinicians, 
leading to skepticism about their practical application.
A recently updated Cochrane review on the effective -
ness of decision support tools showed that they can 
largely increase patients’ knowledge of options, feeling 
informed and clear about what matters most to them, 
and their active role in decision-making [22]. With bet -
ter insights into the risk of experiencing a poor outcome 
or early revision, patients with an elevated risk for a poor 
outcome may be identified and benefit from more inten -
sive follow-up and care processes after THA or TKA. 
Clinical decision-making and health communication 
might also be improved. To the best of our knowledge, no 
study has compared RF and XGBoost with LR for predic -
tion modelling of patient-reported outcomes and risk for 
revision surgery in the orthopaedic literature.
In this study, we aim to compare the predictive 
performance of LR with different machine learning 
approaches for predicting patient outcomes and risk for 
revision surgery after THA and TKA.

Our specific objectives are:
1) To compare the performance of random forest and 
extreme gradient boosting algorithms with a logistic 
regression‐based model for predicting risk for poor 
patient-reported outcomes (pain intensity and satis -
faction) one year after THA or TKA.
2) To compare the performance of random forest and 
extreme gradient boosting algorithms with a logistic 
regression‐based model for predicting risk for revi -
sion surgery within 5 years after THA or TKA.
Methods
Prediction modelling
Our study will be informed by the prognosis research 
study (PROGRESS) framework [13], and reported 
according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Obser -
vational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement 
[23] and the updated Transparent Reporting of a Mul -
tivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or 
Diagnosis (TRIPOD + AI) statement, which provides 
guidance for reporting clinical prediction models that use 
regression or machine learning methods [24, 25].
Study design and data source
A population-based historical cohort study will be con -
ducted using routinely collected data from all primary 
and revision hip and knee arthroplasty procedures per -
formed in Switzerland and registered in SIRIS. To ensure 
the completeness and consistency of SIRIS data, system -
atic software-generated validation tests are conducted 
on the received data. Additionally, on a quarterly basis, 
the registry’s statistician employs an automated analysis 
script to generate master files and lists identifying poten -
tial data errors. These findings are then reviewed by the 
data monitoring team, which conducts root cause analy -
ses for confirmed issues and provides feedback to the 
respective hospitals.
Eligibility criteria
The study population will consist of patients of 
age ≥ 40  years at the time of surgery who were regis -
tered in the SIRIS as having undergone total hip or knee 
arthroplasty for primary osteoarthritis from 01 January 
2015 up to 31 December 2023.
Outcome measures and variables
Objective 1: 12-month postoperative patient-reported 
outcomes of pain intensity and satisfaction will be the 
outcome measures for objective 1. Since 2019, all hos -
pitals receiving public funding for elective hip and 
knee arthroplasties in the Canton of Zurich must col -
lect the Swiss orthopaedic minimal dataset (MDS) [26, 
27] consisting of the 5-dimension, 5-level version of 
the EuroQol health-related quality of life measure (EQ-
5D-5L) and a small selection of additional questions on 
joint-specific pain and patient satisfaction. Question -
naires are collected approximately 1  week preopera -
tively and 12  months postoperatively. Self-rated health 
is assessed using the visual analogue scale (VAS) of the 
EQ-5D-5L ranging from 0 (worst imaginable health) to 
100 (best imaginable health). Joint-specific pain inten -
sity (average over last week) is measured on an 11-point 
numeric rating scale (NRS; 0 being “no pain, ” and 10 
being “the worst pain I can imagine”). Joint-specific sat -
isfaction is assessed on a 5-item scale (1 being “very satis-
fied, ” and 5 being “very dissatisfied”).
In the absence of consensus for a dichotomous defi -
nition of poor response after THA and TKA [28], we 
were guided by the recommendations of an international 
Delphi study and defined poor responders for satisfac -
tion outcome as those who answered “very unsatisfied, ” 
“unsatisfied, ” or “neither satisfied nor unsatisfied” [29]. 
For pain outcome, we defined poor responders as those 
who reported either a relative change of < 50% improve-
ment in pain intensity from preoperative to 1–year post -
operative measurement, or < 3 absolute reduction in pain 
intensity on the 11-point NRS.
Objective 2: The outcome of interest for objective 2 will 
be early revision after THA or TKA. This is defined as 
revision surgery for any indication within five years after 
primary surgery.
Predictor variables
To prespecify a set of predictor variables, we followed the 
approach recommended by Steyerberg et  al. [30]. First, 
candidate predictor variables were identified from the 
available relevant literature. Then, the candidate predic -
tor variables were shared with and discussed in the inter -
disciplinary research group. Final selection of predictor 
variables was made through discussion and consensus of 
all collaborators. The predictor variables that were identi-
fied and that will be included in the model are summa -
rised in Tables  1 and 2. For objective 2, PROMs will not 
be included because they are only collected since 2019, 
which would lead to a substantial decrease of the sample 
size. Nominal categorical variables will be transformed 
to dummy variables using one-hot encoding, and ordinal 
categorical variables will be transformed to numerical 
variables.
Development and external validation cohorts
For poor patient-reported outcome prediction modelling, 
the development and validation cohorts will be formed 
based on the 12 districts of Canton Zurich. The develop -
ment cohort will include the districts of Zürich, Affoltern,

the districts of Horgen, Meilen, Uster, Hinwil, Dielsdorf, 
Bülach, Pfäffikon, Winterthur, and Andelfingen.
For revision risk, the development and validation

Sample size
Following Riley et  al. [51], we calculated the minimum 
sample size using the R package pmsampsize . As rec -
ommended, we set Nagelkerke’s R2 = 0.15 (Cox-Snell’s 
adjusted R2 = 0.09) and the assumed shrinkage factor 
equal to 0.9.
For the prediction of poor patient-reported outcome 
after THA, with an observed prevalence of approximately 
9.8% for pain and 7.2% for satisfaction in our study popu-
lation and 20 predictor parameters, 2434 participants are 
required for poor pain outcome and 2850 for poor satis -
faction outcome.
For the prediction of poor patient-reported outcome 
after TKA, with an observed prevalence of approximately 
16.3% for pain and 15.1% for satisfaction and 20 predictor 
parameters, 1944 participants are required for poor pain 
outcome and 1992 for poor satisfaction outcome.
For the prediction of revision surgery within 5  years 
after THA, with an observed prevalence of approxi -
mately 2.7% and 23 predictor parameters, 6158 partici -
pants are required. For the prediction of revision surgery 
within 5 years after TKA, with an observed prevalence of 
approximately 3.9% and 25 predictor parameters, 5232 
participants are required.
Our fixed sample sizes exceed these minimum require -
ments for the development cohorts. Therefore, we 
expect our study to be adequately powered. For exter -
nal validation studies of prognostic models, it has been 
recommended that the validation cohort has a mini -
mum of 100 (or better 200) events [30, 52]. All validation

Missing data
If an outcome measurement is missing, the patient 
data will be excluded from the analysis. Around 60% of 
patients had to be excluded because of missing pain or 
satisfaction outcome. All populations with or without 
patient-reported outcome were comparable (see Supple -
mentary appendix eTables 1 and 2). Predictors with miss-
ingness exceeding 30% will be excluded. Missing data 
among the remaining predictors will be assumed missing 
at random. We will perform multiple imputations using 
the mice package in R, which imputes incomplete multi -
variate data by chained equations algorithm [30, 53]. Five 
imputed datasets will be generated. The model will be 
developed in each imputed dataset and averaged over the 
estimated predictions.
Since for some patients the study ends before poten -
tially undergoing revision surgery, the data will include 
right-censored observations. To address this, Kaplan–
Meier imputation will be performed using the CondiS 
package in R. This method imputes survival times for 
censored cases based on their conditional survival distri -
butions, derived from the Kaplan–Meier estimator [54].
Model development
We will develop the prediction models based on the “full 
model approach, ” which is using the pre-selected prog -
nostic factors without any being excluded at a later stage. 
To avoid loss of prognostic information, continuous vari-
ables will be kept continuous and not categorised [55].
LR model
We will use multivariable logistic regression to develop 
a prediction model for the incidence of revision surgery 
within 5 years after primary surgery. For predicting poor 
patient-reported outcome, outcomes of interest will be 
dichotomized.
To account for the risk of overfitting, an additional 
penalized logistic regression model will be developed 
using the glmnet package in R. Elastic Net regression, a 
combination of Ridge and least absolute shrinkage and 
selection operator (LASSO) regression, will be used.
RF and XGBoost model
Both RF and XGBoost algorithms are commonly used 
for prediction modelling in the area of THA and TKA 
[20]. RF operates by constructing a multitude of decision 
trees during the training phase, each using a subset of the 
development data. The individual predictions of all trees 
are aggregated to produce the final output. Compared 
to RF, XGBoost creates decision trees sequentially, with 
each new tree aiming to correct the errors made by the 
previous ensemble. It uses a gradient-based optimization 
approach to guide the tree-building process. Grid search 
will be used for hyperparameter tuning.
Internal validation
Five-fold repeated cross-validation will be used to 
account for optimism due to model overfitting [56]. After 
splitting development cohort data into five equally big 
parts, four parts are used as training set, and one part 
retained for testing model performance once model 
training is finished. This will be iterated until each of the 
parts will have been used for validation once. Random 
seeds will be set to make results reproducible.
Evaluation and comparison of model performance
The predictive performance of the developed models 
will be assessed and compared using measures of over -
all performance, calibration, and discrimination. To 
quantify overall classification performance, the distance 
between the predicted and actual outcomes will be evalu-
ated using accuracy calculated in the confusion matrix. 
We define accuracy as the proportion of the predictions 
that are correct. The calibration ability of the developed 
model for the agreement between the estimated prob -
abilities of the outcome and the observed outcome’s pro -
portion will be assessed via a calibration plot with loess 
smoother. To explore the model’s ability to discriminate 
between those with and those without the outcome, the 
concordance (c) statistic will be used. For prediction 
models with binary outcomes, c is identical to the area 
under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC). 
The area under the curve (AUC) can be understood as 
the probability that the model assigns a higher probability 
of the outcome to a patient with the outcome compared 
to a randomly selected patient without the outcome [57]. 
Variable importance will be used to indicate how much 
each variable contributes to the predictive performance 
of all models using the varImp function from the caret 
package in R.
All analyses will be performed using R version 4.3.0 (R 
Foundation for Statistical Computing) [58].
Clinical usefulness of prediction models
We will perform decision curve analysis to evaluate the 
clinical utility and better understand whether the clini -
cal use of a prediction model would do more good than 
harm [59]. By examining model performance against 
standardized net benefit and risk thresholds, one can 
assess how deciding to perform an intervention for an 
individual patient at their specific risk level, as indicated 
by the prediction model, compares with other manage -
ment options such as performing an intervention for all

patients or no patients [60]. Our decision curve analysis 
will consider two clinically relevant scenarios: (1) Should 
total joint replacement surgery be recommended to 
patients who are at the decision point for surgical inter -
vention? (2) Should patients who undergo total joint 
replacement surgery be provided more intense rehabilita-
tion (e.g., increased pre- and postoperative rehabilitation 
programs) as a preventive measure to mitigate the risk for 
poor outcomes? Our decision curves will present the net 
benefit of our prediction model versus two default deci -
sions that should always be present in a decision curve: 
treating none (with a net benefit of zero) and treating all.
External validation
We will externally validate our models using geographi -
cal validation as described before [30]. The performance 
of the models in the external validation cohorts will be 
assessed using measures of overall performance, discrim -
ination, and calibration.
Model presentation
The final regression model consisting of the development 
and validation cohort will be presented using intercept 
to present baseline risk, regression coefficients, and odds 
ratios with 95% confidence intervals.
Discussion
We have described a protocol for developing, validat -
ing, and comparing traditional LR with different machine 
learning approaches for predicting patient outcomes 
and risk for revision surgery after THA and TKA using 
a large population-based cohort derived from a Swiss 
national joint registry. Prediction models as decision sup-
port tools may support preoperative clinical decision-
making, health resource allocation, and care planning. 
To our knowledge, no risk prediction model using Swiss 
national data has been published in this area, and other 
studies comparing the predictive performance of ML 
algorithms are poorly reported [19].
A key strength of our study using registry data is the 
availability of relevant and comprehensive routine clini -
cal data across a wide population, which enhances the 
generalizability and robustness of the prediction models. 
Limitations include the use of retrospective data collec -
tion, which limits the type of information that can be 
considered and included in the prediction models. Since 
patient-reported outcomes are only collected since 2019 
in Canton Zurich, the study population for prediction of 
poor patient-reported outcomes will be limited to this 
population.
