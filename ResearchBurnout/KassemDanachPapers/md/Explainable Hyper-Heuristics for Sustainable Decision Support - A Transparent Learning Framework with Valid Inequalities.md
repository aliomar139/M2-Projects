Introduction** The growing complexity of modern supply chains and production systems has amplified the need for optimization frameworks that jointly pursue operational efficiency and sustainability. As organizations face volatile market conditions, stringent environmental regulations, and increasing expectations for fairness and transparency, classical scheduling and routing models are being reshaped into richer, multi-objective formulations capable of balancing economic performance with environmental and social responsibility. At the same time, advances in metaheuristics, hyperheuristics, mathematical model tightening, and explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) have opened new avenues for adaptive and interpretable decision-making. Yet, these developments have largely evolved in parallel, leaving open the question of how to integrate rigorous mathematical foundations, data-driven heuristic selection, and transparent reasoning into a unified methodology for sustainable operations. This section provides the conceptual background and motivation for such an integration, tracing the progression from traditional heuristics to explainable hyperheuristic frameworks grounded in strengthened optimization models. Sustainable operations management increasingly demands the joint optimization of _efficiency_ —capturing cost, time, and productivity—and _sustainability_ —reflecting CO2 emissions, energy consumption, and workload equity. The dual imperative of performance and responsibility has reshaped classical scheduling and routing problems into multi-objective frameworks that explicitly trade off economic and environmental criteria Deb, Pratap, Agarwal and Meyarivan (2002); Zhang and Li (2007); Bektas and Laporte (2011b); Asghari, Mirzapour Al-e Hashem and Karimi (2021). Traditional metaheuristics, including genetic algorithms (GA), simulated annealing (SA), and ant colony optimization (ACO), have long been effective for large-scale combinatorial problems Eiben and Smith (2003); Gendreau and Potvin (2019). Yet, they often exhibit brittle behavior under regime shifts such as tariff surges, carbon-tax changes, or demand disruptions—conditions that are increasingly common in modern supply chains. Furthermore, most metaheuristics operate as opaque systems: they deliver feasible and often high-quality solutions but provide limited insight into the _decision rationale_ behind each search move or heuristic selection. Hyper-heuristics (HHs) emerged as a higher-level strategy to control a set of low-level heuristics through adaptive learning Burke, Kendall and Soubeiga (2003). Unlike single-operator heuristics, HHs aim to generalize across domains by learning how to select and apply heuristics according to instance features or search-state information.
