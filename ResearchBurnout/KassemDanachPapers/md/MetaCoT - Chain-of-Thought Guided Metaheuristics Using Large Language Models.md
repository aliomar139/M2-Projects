Background and Related Work 2.1. Metaheuristic Foundations** Metaheuristic algorithms form the cornerstone of modern approaches to NP-hard optimization problems, providing flexible frameworks that balance exploration and exploitation. These methods typically employ a set of core operators that guide the search process through the solution space. The fundamental operators in population-based metaheuristics such as Genetic Algorithms (GA) include Crossover (), Mutation (), and Selection (). These operators are traditionally applied with fixed or predetermined parameters, which limits their adaptability to varying search conditions. For instance, crossover  combines genetic material from parent solutions with a fixed probability _𝑝𝑐_ , while mutation  introduces random perturbations with probability _𝑝𝑚_ . Selection  determines which solutions survive to the next generation based on fitness evaluation. To address the limitations of static operators, researchers have developed adaptive variants that dynamically adjust parameters during the search process. These parameter control methods (Eiben and Smith, 1999) can be classified into three categories: deterministic, adaptive, and self-adaptive. Deterministic methods follow predetermined schedules, such as the cooling schedule in Simulated Annealing (Kirkpatrick, Gelatt and Vecchi, 1983). Adaptive methods adjust parameters based on search feedback, such as diversity measures or convergence rate (Eiben and Smith, 1999). Selfadaptive methods encode parameters within the solution representation and evolve them alongside solutions, as seen in evolution strategies (Beyer and Deb, 2002). Chain-of-Thought Reasoning** Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning represents a significant advancement in the capabilities of Large Language Models, enabling them to break down complex problems into intermediate steps that mirror human cognitive processes. Introduced by Wei et al Wei et al. (2022), CoT prompting encourages LLMs to generate step-by-step reasoning before arriving at a final answer, dramatically improving performance on tasks requiring logical deduction, mathematical reasoning, and commonsense understanding. This approach leverages the inherent structure of complex problems, allowing models to decompose them into more manageable subproblems and solve them sequentially. The effectiveness of CoT reasoning has been demonstrated across a wide range of domains, from arithmetic word problems (Khot et al., 2023) to commonsense reasoning (Wei et al., 2022). By generating explicit reasoning trajectories, CoT provides transparency into the decision-making process of LLMs, making their outputs more interpretable and trustworthy. This transparency is particularly valuable in optimization contexts, where understanding the rationale behind algorithmic decisions can inform further improvements and build user confidence. Recent extensions of CoT reasoning include techniques such as self-consistency, which samples multiple reasoning paths and selects the most consistent answer (Wang, Wei, Schuurmans, Le, Chi and Zhou, 2022), and tree-ofthought, which explores multiple reasoning possibilities in a tree structure (Yao, Yu, Zhao, Shafran, Griffiths, Cao and Narasimhan, 2023). These advancements highlight the potential of structured reasoning approaches to enhance the problem-solving capabilities of LLMs. Our work builds upon this foundation by applying CoT reasoning to the domain of metaheuristic optimization, where the ability to generate context-aware adaptation strategies can significantly improve search performance. **2.3. LLMs in Optimization** The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) has opened new possibilities for enhancing optimization algorithms through natural language understanding and generation. Current approaches to integrating LLMs in optimization can be broadly categorized into two main streams: direct LLM solvers and LLMs as heuristic generators. Direct LLM solvers attempt to formulate optimization problems as natural language prompts and rely on the LLM’s inherent reasoning capabilities to generate solutions. While promising for small instances, this approach faces significant scalability challenges due to LLMs’ token limitations and computational complexity. For example, Chen at al. Chen et al. (2023) demonstrated that LLMs can solve small TSP instances but struggle with problems exceeding 20 nodes. Similarly, Yang et al. Yang et al. (2023) explored the use of LLMs for combinatorial optimization, finding that while they can provide reasonable solutions for small-scale problems, their performance degrades rapidly as problem size increases. The second stream leverages LLMs as heuristic generators, where the models extract or create problem-specific rules that guide traditional optimization algorithms. Zhou et al Zhou et al. (2023) used LLMs to decompose arithmetic word problems into manageable subproblems, demonstrating the potential of LLMs in problem restructuring. Recent work has also explored the use of LLMs for algorithm selection and configuration, where models recommend appropriate optimization algorithms or parameters based on problem characteristics (Hutter, Hoos and Leyton-Brown, 2014).
